RandomBench

How well can language models pick numbers at random?

Each model was asked many times for a random integer from 0 to 99, in a fresh conversation every time. A model that gives the same answer every time scores 0.01, and a perfect random number generator scores about 1.

Answers draws how often the model gave each value, with its favorite in orange. The bars follow the square root of the counts, so a value given a quarter as often as the favorite is half as tall, and one given only once still shows.

Replies counts every call that got a reply, valid or not. Calls that failed, such as those the quota refused, aren't counted. Every score's 95% interval comes from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of the model's replies, so a model with fewer replies gets a wider interval, not a lower score.

A rank is a range where scores are too close to call. A model ranks below every model whose interval lies wholly above its own, and above every model whose interval lies wholly below it. Where two intervals overlap, either model could be ahead. Each range compares the models two at a time, so with this many models, an end of a range may now and then be off by one.

The bias checks on a share of the answers, such as Multiples of 10, are lenient toward avoidance. A model that never answers a multiple of 10 is only 10% away from the ideal share, so it scores 0.900, and one that answers nothing else scores 0.100.

What stands out

No model comes close. Even the most random model scores less than 0.2, and the top of its interval is less than 0.2 too. A perfect random number generator scores about 1.

Three numbers dominate. All but five models favor 42, 47, or 73, and those three numbers make up more than 70% of all the answers. Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite and Claude Opus 4.6 have answered nothing but 42, and five more models give their favorite more than 85% of the time: Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Claude Sonnet 5 and 5.5. Two of the exceptions, gpt-oss-120b and GPT-5.4, favor 57. The other three are the newest models here, and they break away. GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol both favor 63, and give 42, 47, or 73 less than a third of the time. Gemini 4 Neon favors 68, and gives those three numbers less than 2% of the time. All three rank among the six most random models.

Newer isn't more random. GPT-5.4 ranks first, ahead of every later GPT, from GPT-5.5 to GPT-6.2. Gemini 2.5 Flash, one of the oldest models here, has the second-highest score, ahead of every newer Gemini. Grok 4.7 is less random than Grok 4.6, GPT-6 Luna than GPT-5.6 Luna, and Claude Sonnet 5 and 5.5 than Sonnet 4.6. Some go the other way: each Sol model is more random than the one before, from GPT-5.6 Sol to GPT-6.2 Sol. Gemini 4 Neon is more random than Gemini 4 Argon, and Claude Haiku 5.5 than Haiku 4.5. Claude Opus 5.5 is the most random Claude. None of these comparisons has overlapping intervals, except Gemini 4 Neon's with Gemini 2.5 Flash, whose scores are too close to tell apart.

Whole regions go unused. More than 95% of all answers lie between 20 and 79, and more than 90% end in 2, 3, or 7. Gemini 4 Neon is the exception: fewer than a third of its answers end in 2, 3, or 7, and more end in 8 than in any other digit. Only GPT-5.4, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Grok 4.6, three of the four most random models, have answered above 89 or with a repeated digit like 77. Only GPT-5.4 and Grok 4.6 have answered below 10, and only GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, and GPT-5.6 Terra with a multiple of 10. Every other model has avoided all of these, and multiples of 5 too, so they all get the same score on Multiples of 10, Multiples of 5, Edges, and Repeated Digits. Here the identical scores are the finding.

A share check can pass by accident. gpt-oss-120b scores close to perfect on primes. Its share of primes is near the ideal 25%, but it comes from its three favorites, 57, 42, and 73, of which only 73 is prime. Every model whose favorite is 47 or 73, both prime, answers with a prime more than half the time, twice what chance would give. GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Sol, and Claude Sonnet 5 and 5.5 do more than 94% of the time.

Nearly every reply is valid. The one big exception is Claude Opus 4.8 when it's asked to draw a raffle winner. It declines nearly every time, saying it can't draw a truly random number, and suggests a better way, such as random.org, a spreadsheet's RANDBETWEEN, or Python. A few of those replies offer a casual pick anyway, and some warn that it would favor numbers like 37, 42, or 73. Its other replies are all valid. Grok 4.6 now and then adds a confidence score after its answer, as in 47\n\confidence{80}, and the second number makes the reply invalid. Claude Opus 5.5 has slipped five times: once with an empty reply, and four times by writing out its thinking, three of them before the number and one with no number at all. Every other model's replies have all been valid.

Reasoning helps a little, but doesn't fix it. Eleven models reason at length by default, spending a median of more than 50 output tokens on a two-digit answer, and Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Grok 4.6 more than 200. The models that spend more tokens tend to score higher, but the most random, GPT-5.4, answers in a median of 4. It isn't a clean comparison, since reasoning goes with provider: all eleven are from Google or xAI, or are gpt-oss-120b. And the models that reason are still far from uniform: none scores above 0.12 on Uniformity, where a fair pick would score close to 1.

Asking for a uniform pick helps one model a lot. Each model was asked in six wordings, and one asks for an integer chosen uniformly at random, so that every value is equally likely. Asked that way, GPT-5.4 scores more than 0.4 on Uniformity, more than three times its score on the other five wordings together, and it scores higher that way in every one of its runs. No other model's score even triples. Across all the models, the uniform wording raises Uniformity for only about half of them.
